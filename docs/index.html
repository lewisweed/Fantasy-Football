Five Thousand Seasons

We rebuilt the 2025 fantasy season inside a computer and played it five thousand times, with twelve managers drafting, streaming and setting lineups in every one. Here is what actually won — and how much of it was luck.

How to read the numbers

Four ideas do all the work on this page. None of them are complicated, but every chart below assumes you know them.

Fair share — 8.33%

Twelve teams, one champion. If every manager were identical, each would win 1÷12 = 8.33% of the time. That is the line to beat. A strategy at 6% is losing to a manager who does nothing in particular; a strategy at 10% is genuinely ahead.

Confidence interval

The range the true number probably sits in, written like 9.9–11.7%. We only played 5,000 seasons, not infinite ones, so every figure has some wobble. If two intervals overlap, the two things are not meaningfully different — that is the single most useful habit for reading this page.

Counterfactual

Replaying the same league with one thing changed. We re-ran each season with one manager forced to draft a quarterback in a set round, everything else untouched, and compared him to himself. It is the difference between “teams that did X won more” and “doing X makes you win more” — which are not the same claim.

Variance explained

How much of the outcome a factor actually accounts for. If draft strategy explains 10% of your season, the other 90% is injuries, breakouts, and who you happened to play each week. It is the honest ceiling on advice.

Question one

Which draft strategy wins?

Each simulated manager drafts with a persona — a tendency, not a rule. A Zero RB manager avoids running backs early but will still take one if the value is obvious, exactly like a real person would.

Zero RB didn’t just lose finals. It missed the playoffs.

Zero RB made the playoffs 39.9% of the time against roughly 50% for everyone else. It lost in the regular season, which means the strategy failed — not that it ran into bad luck in December.

The spread from best to worst here is wider than anything the quarterback tables produce. Whatever you take from this page, how you build the rest of the roster mattered more than when you took a quarterback.

Question two

When should you draft a quarterback?

This is the question the project was built for, and it is the one where the obvious answer is wrong in an interesting way.

The tempting approach is to sort teams by when they took their first quarterback and compare. Do that and you get a clean story: rounds 2–3 won 5.98% of titles, rounds 10 and later won 9.90%. Wait, obviously.

But that comparison is contaminated. The managers who reach for a quarterback in round 2 are also the managers doing other things differently, and those other things are in the number too. To separate them you have to replay the same league and change only one thing.

The mistake is the early reach, not the timing after it.

Round 5 looks like the peak, but rounds 4 through 12 all sit inside each other’s intervals. Anywhere in that range is fine. Rounds 2 and 3 are not.

There is a second clue in the raw data that points the same way. Teams that spent a round-2 pick on a quarterback got 18.2 points a game out of the position. Teams that waited until round 10 or later got 17.6. Paying up bought about half a point a game. It cost a pick that could have been a starting running back.

Question two, continued

Which quarterbacks were worth it?

The same question asked player by player: what did he cost, and what did the teams that drafted him actually do?

Josh Allen is the clearest case on the page. He scored 21.9 points a game, the most of any quarterback in the simulation, and did exactly what you drafted him to do. Teams that opened with him still won just 7.28% of titles — below the 8.33% you get for showing up. The pick worked and the team still lost ground, because a round-2 pick spent on a quarterback is a running back or receiver you do not have.

Drake Maye, typically available around round 11, delivered 19.3 points a game and a 13.25% title rate — the best on the board, at a twentieth of the cost.

The thing nobody drafts for

Paying attention beat every draft strategy

This is the least glamorous finding and probably the most useful one. The draft is a single afternoon that everyone agonises over; the waiver wire is fourteen small decisions that most people skip.

A trap worth naming

Draft slot mattered enormously — and tells you nothing

Teams picking 9th through 12th won 10.34% of titles. Teams picking 5th through 8th won 6.62%. That gap is far too large to be noise, and it is completely useless as advice.

Here is why. Every one of these 5,000 leagues replays the same 2025 season — the same players get hurt in the same weeks. In 2025, picks 5 through 8 were Justin Jefferson, Jahmyr Gibbs and Malik Nabers. Once the fantasy playoffs arrived, those three scored at 0.76, 0.59 and 0.00 times their own earlier pace. Nabers had torn his ACL.

We checked that this was the season and not the format: measured on draft day, expected value is flat across all twelve slots, varying by 1.9%. The snake is fair. 2025 simply wasn’t.

A large, confidently-measured effect that will never repeat.

This is the clearest illustration on the page of why a single season — even one played 5,000 times — cannot tell you what to do next year.

The honest part

How much of this actually matters?

Put draft persona, quarterback timing and manager effort together and they account for about 10% of the variation in how much a team scores. The other 90% is which players got hurt, which unknowns broke out, and who you drew each week.

We tested what that ceiling means in practice. Rank twelve teams by strategy alone, then compare that ranking to how they really finished. Across thousands of leagues the agreement averages 0.21 on a scale where 1 is perfect and 0 is random — and it swings by ±0.29, so a completely backwards prediction is a normal outcome.

What that means for your league

Everything on this page is real: the differences held up across 5,000 leagues, two different waiver systems and three different mixes of opponents. But they are small compared to one year of football. A manager following every recommendation here will still lose most seasons, and the manager who ignores all of it will sometimes win. The advice shifts the odds; it does not decide the season.

Method

How the simulation works

Every league is a complete season played from the draft to the final, using the real 2025 numbers.

  1. Draft. Sixteen rounds, snake order, random slots. Each manager reads the board through his own noise, so nobody has the same rankings. Draft costs come from Fantasy Football Calculator’s record of 8,470 real mock drafts.
  2. Play the weeks. Scoring, weekly projections and player availability are ESPN’s own, cross-checked against official injury reports and NFL rosters.
  3. Work the wire. Every week, managers value their squad, set lineups, stash the injured and submit waiver claims under ESPN’s real priority rules. They judge a move by whether the roster improves, not by comparing two players in isolation — which is why they keep handcuffs and bench depth.
  4. Finish the season. Fourteen head-to-head weeks, then a six-team playoff with byes for the top two seeds.

Nothing knows the future

The hardest part was keeping hindsight out. Projection sources quietly blank out a player’s week once he is known to have missed it — including the week he got hurt mid-game. Joe Burrow’s week 2 arrived showing a projection of zero and seven points actually scored. Left alone, that hands every simulated manager a warning nobody had on the Sunday morning. Those weeks are repaired; a manager only knows a player is out when the official injury report or the roster said so before kickoff.

Checks it had to pass

  • Simulated draft positions match real mock-draft data at r = 0.93, within about 5 picks across the top 100.
  • 20.4 quarterbacks drafted per league, matching real 12-team behaviour.
  • Teams score 112 points a week, which is what an ESPN PPR league looks like.
  • 2025’s busts get dropped (Joe Mixon 99% of the time) and its breakouts get picked up.

Caveats

What this can’t tell you

  • It is one season, played many times. The variety is in drafts, schedules and waiver runs — not in football. A strategy that looks good here may only be good at 2025. Running 2023 and 2024 is what separates a real edge from one year’s luck.
  • The managers are model managers. They don’t trade, don’t read beat reports and don’t tilt. Their disagreements are statistical noise rather than genuinely different opinions about football.
  • Championship rates are noisy by nature. One champion per league means even 5,000 seasons gives only a few hundred titles per strategy. Season points and playoff rate are measured far better — prefer them when ranking.
  • The mix of opponents is an assumption, built from published 2025 draft advice rather than observed from real leagues. Every finding here was re-run against different mixes; anything that moved between them was dropped.
