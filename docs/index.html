A fantasy football experiment · seven seasons

Five Thousand Seasons

We rebuilt seven real fantasy seasons inside a computer and played each of them five thousand times, with twelve managers drafting, streaming and setting lineups in every one. We went looking for the best draft strategy. What we found instead is that the answer changes every year — and nothing you can see in August tells you which year you are about to play.

Before anything else

How to read the numbers

Four terms do most of the work on this page. None of them are complicated, but guessing at them will make the conclusions look either stronger or weaker than they are.

Standing (z‑score)

How far a strategy’s teams scored above or below the league average that season, measured in standard deviations. 0 is exactly average; +1.0 means a full standard deviation better than the field. We use it instead of raw points because a point is worth more in a low-scoring year.

Confidence interval

The range the true answer plausibly sits in. If an interval crosses zero, the result is consistent with no effect at all — the honest reading is “we cannot tell,” not “slightly better.”

Rank agreement

Whether two seasons order the strategies the same way. +1 is identical, 0 is unrelated, −1 is exactly backwards. This is the number that settles whether a strategy travels from one year to the next.

Points vs projection

What a player actually scored minus what he was projected to score in August. It is pure hindsight, used only to explain results. No simulated manager ever sees it.

One rule governs the whole simulation: every manager decides using only what was knowable at that moment. They do not know who gets hurt in week 9. They do not know which rookie breaks out. They draft from August rankings, react to injuries as they are reported, and work the waiver wire with the same blind spots a real person has.

The cast

The eleven playbooks

Every simulated manager drafts with one of these. They are tendencies, not rules — a playbook nudges the close calls, and a big enough gap in value overrides it every time, exactly as it would for a person.

Here is the proof that these are tendencies and not scripts. Zero RB is defined by avoiding running backs — and it still takes its first one in round 7, with five more after that. When the board hands a manager something too good to pass up, he takes it and adjusts. A strategy that never deviated would be a program, not a person.

The finding

The best strategy changes every year — violently

This is the chart the project was built to produce, and it is not the chart we expected. Each line is one strategy, tracked across seven seasons. If any strategy were reliably good, its line would stay high.

Zero RB is the best strategy in the dataset and the worst strategy in the dataset. In 2018 it finished +1.79, first of eleven. In 2019 it finished −3.06, last by a mile. Robust RB does the same thing in reverse: worst in 2023, best in 2025.

The sharpest case arrived with the two seasons added last, and it is consecutive. QB streamer is the best strategy of 2021 and the worst of 2022. Elite TE + early QB does the exact reverse over the same two years. A manager who drew the obvious lesson from 2021 — don’t pay for a quarterback — was running the worst board in the league twelve months later.

These are not small samples wobbling. Within any single season each of these gaps is measured across tens of thousands of teams and is extremely precise. The lines really do cross.

Not one of the eleven strategies has a seven-season average distinguishable from the field.

Including Zero RB, which is worst overall by a distance — and whose interval still reaches +0.86, because in 2018 it was the best strategy on the board.

Is the ranking just noise?

No, and this is the part that makes the finding sharp. We measured the same thing two ways.

Do seasons agree with each other? Rank agreement across all 21 pairs of seasons averages −0.09, and between 2018 and 2024 it is −0.84 — nearly a perfect reversal. Shuffling eleven strategies at random gives 0.00 ± 0.32, so this is indistinguishable from no relationship at all.

Does a season agree with itself? Split one season’s leagues into two halves and rank the strategies separately in each. Agreement averages +0.92.

So the measurement is nearly perfect, and it still does not travel. A season’s ranking is real, precise, and about that season. That is the cleanest way this question can be settled with the data available.

The mechanism

What actually decides a season

If the winner changes every year, something must be changing. It is not subtle: it is whether the running backs or the receivers taken in the first five rounds lived up to their projections.

That last column explains almost everything. Correlate it with how each strategy did:

  • Robust RB: +0.93. Its season is decided by that one number almost entirely.
  • Zero RB: −0.74. The same bet, taken from the other side.
  • Balanced: +0.76. Worth naming honestly — a manager who just follows consensus inherits a running-back-heavy early board in this era, and carries much of the same exposure without ever choosing it.

Robust RB and Zero RB are not really strategies. They are leveraged bets on one number.

A number that is not knowable until December.

The question that would make this useful

Can you tell in August which season you are in?

If you could, everything above becomes actionable: read the board, pick the matching strategy, win. So we tested it properly, on more seasons than we could simulate.

We profiled fourteen draft boards, 2010 through 2025, on nine things a manager can see before drafting: how many running backs and receivers go in the top 24, where the first quarterback and the first tight end leave the board, and how steep each position’s tier cliff is. Then we asked whether any of them predicted how that season actually paid off.

One of the nine cleared the usual bar: the receiver tier cliff, at r = −0.63. Taken alone that reads as significant.

But nine things were tested. When you run nine tests, the chance that at least one clears that bar by luck alone is about 37%, and this one does not survive the standard correction for having done so. It is a hypothesis worth checking against future seasons, not a result.

Market pricing fails the same test independently: across those fourteen seasons, the correlation between what a position cost in August and what it returned runs between −0.35 and +0.08, none of them significant.

Nothing observable before the draft predicts which kind of season you are about to play.

Which is exactly why the strategy that wins keeps changing.

The stories behind the swings

Season by season, with names

Averages hide the thing worth understanding. Each of these seasons was decided by a handful of players, and in almost every case by something nobody could have priced in August.

2018 — the holdout that made the season · won by Hero RB, +1.87

Le’Veon Bell was the third pick of the average draft and scored nothing at all. He sat out the entire season in a franchise-tag dispute and never reported — something no projection can price, because holdouts of that length essentially do not happen. Behind him the whole early running-back board came apart: Devonta Freeman (10 games missed), Dalvin Cook, Leonard Fournette, LeSean McCoy. Early backs finished 38 points under projection while receivers lost only 9.

The twist: the single largest windfall anywhere in the dataset was James Conner — Bell’s own backup — who went at pick 157 and scored 215 points above projection. The event that destroyed Robust RB created the season’s best value, and it was sitting there for anyone in the last third of the draft.

Hero RB won by holding one back instead of three and having the roster space to absorb Conner. Zero RB posted its best season ever at +1.79 — the single reason it cannot simply be called a bad strategy.

2019 — the flat board that still sank Zero RB · won by Late QB, +0.78

The most instructive season in the set, because the board was level: early backs finished 31 points under projection, early receivers 32. Neither position had an edge. And Zero RB still recorded −3.06, the worst single-season result of any strategy in five years.

The reason matters. Zero RB does not need backs to bust in order to win — it needs receivers to outperform, and in 2019 they did not. Antonio Brown, drafted 21st on average, played one game. Davante Adams was the strategy’s single largest loss. A roster that concedes a position with two starting slots and a flex has to be paid somewhere; on a neutral board it is not, and it loses badly.

Late QB won on one player: Lamar Jackson, available at pick 98, who produced a unanimous-MVP season worth 110 points over projection. Waiting at quarterback is a good idea precisely when someone like that is sitting in round eight — and there is no way to know in August that he is.

2023 — winning by not being there · won by Elite QB only, +1.31

The worst running-back season in the set, 61 points under projection, and the damage was injury rather than underperformance: Nick Chubb’s knee in week 2, J.K. Dobbins’ Achilles in week 1, Jonathan Taylor and Aaron Jones six games each. Justin Jefferson, the first pick of the average draft, missed seven.

This is the cleanest example in the project of winning by avoidance. Every one of Elite QB only’s five largest contributions is a player it did not have: Aaron Rodgers, who tore his Achilles four snaps into the season, Deshaun Watson, Anthony Richardson, Daniel Jones. By spending a round 2–4 pick on a top quarterback it was never shopping in the aisle where every mid-tier option detonated. It did not find a bargain. It simply was not standing there when the floor gave way.

2024 — the distinction that matters most · won by Robust RB, +1.49

A mirror of 2018. Early backs finished exactly on projection and early receivers 40 points under — the most RB-friendly board of the five.

And here is the nuance this whole report turns on. The single biggest bust of 2024 was a running back: Christian McCaffrey, first pick of the average draft, 228 points under projection with nine games missed. A reader who knew only that would conclude 2024 punished running backs. The opposite is true — the class delivered, with Barkley, Henry, Gibbs, Jacobs and Montgomery all paying off and Chuba Hubbard and Bucky Irving arriving off the wire.

“The top running back busted” and “running backs were a bad investment” are different claims, and only the second has anything to do with strategy. The real damage was at receiver — Chris Olave, Brandon Aiyuk, Christian Kirk — which is exactly where Zero RB had spent its early picks.

2025 — the season that misled us · won by Robust RB, +1.60

The most quarterback-hostile season of the five by a wide margin: early quarterbacks finished 88 points under projection, roughly double the next-worst year. Joe Burrow and Jayden Daniels, both going in round 3, were the two largest busts anywhere in the first five rounds, at −214 and −192. Between them they account for more than the whole of Early QB’s season deficit.

Receivers were nearly as bad, 60 points under, with Malik Nabers, Tyreek Hill, Mike Evans, Terry McLaurin and Calvin Ridley all missing six games or more. Running backs were poor in absolute terms but 19 points better than receivers, and the waiver crop was extraordinary — Rico Dowdle, Quinshon Judkins and Kyle Monangai were all essentially free.

An earlier version of this page was built on 2025 alone. It advised drafting a quarterback late and called Zero RB unambiguously bad. Both statements describe 2025 accurately. Neither survives contact with 2018.

The one thing that held

Paying attention worked every single year

Set this chart next to the first one. The strategy lines cross constantly. These three never touch, in any of the seven seasons.

Active beats moderate beats lazy in all seven seasons, on both title rate and total scoring — twenty-one orderings out of twenty-one. The scoring gap runs from 61 to 105 points a season.

Now compare that to the table further up, where not one draft strategy separated from the field over the same seven years. With more seasons the gap between the best and worst strategy actually shrank, while this one held.

How you draft is a bet on the season. Whether you manage the roster afterwards is not.

This is the least glamorous finding in the project and comfortably the most useful. The draft is one afternoon everybody agonises over; the waiver wire is fourteen small decisions most people skip.

The original question

Where should you draft a quarterback?

This is what the project was built to answer, and the five-season view changes the answer from a rule into a warning.

The simulation’s cleanest test replays identical leagues — same players, same injuries, same opponents — changing only the round in which one team is forced to take its first quarterback. In 2025 that test says: do not pay up. Forcing a quarterback in round 2 cost 18 points a season against the same manager’s normal behaviour; waiting until round 5 gained 17.

Then we measured the same thing across fourteen real draft boards, 2010 to 2025, by asking how much more the quarterbacks taken early returned than the ones taken late.

2025 is the second-worst quarterback season in fifteen years. Only 2015 was worse. In twelve of the fourteen seasons, paying up for a quarterback was worth it, sometimes enormously — +140 in 2022, +139 in 2011.

So the honest version of the advice is not “wait on quarterback.” It is this: quarterback is the only position where the premium has ever failed to pay, which makes it the position where paying up carries real risk — and 2025 happened to be one of the two seasons where that risk landed. Burrow and Daniels, both round-3 picks, were the two biggest busts in the entire first five rounds.

A rule read off 2025 would have told you to wait. It would have been wrong in twelve of the previous thirteen years.

This is the single clearest demonstration on the page of why one season cannot produce a rule.

A correction

Draft slot: the one place more seasons changed our answer

The single-season version of this page said draft slot produced a big effect that meant nothing. With seven seasons, half of that is still right and half of it is not.

Still right: any individual season’s slot pattern is mostly that season. 2024 punished the front of the draft brutally (slot 1 won 2.6% of titles) because Christian McCaffrey was the first pick and missed nine games. In 2023 slot 1 was among the best. You cannot read one year’s slot table and learn anything.

Now wrong: the claim that slot tells you nothing at all. Averaged over seven seasons the back four slots win 10.26% of titles against the front four’s 7.52%, and the middle is consistently the worst place to sit — slot 6 averages 6.98%. The ends of a snake draft get their picks in pairs; the middle never does.

We checked that this is not a bug in how teams are assigned: personas and manager types are distributed evenly across all twelve slots to within sampling noise.

What we still have not established is why. The scoring evidence firmed up with two more seasons — the back of the draft now outscores the front in five of seven, where at five seasons it was three of five — but it is still not unanimous. A mechanism that always moves titles and only usually moves points is not one we can claim to understand. Seven seasons is enough to be confident the effect is real; it is not enough to explain it, and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

If your league lets you pick a slot, the back of the snake is the better bet. Everything else about slot is noise.

And you almost certainly cannot pick.

Under the hood

How the simulation works

Each of the seven seasons is replayed five thousand times. Players score exactly what they really scored, week by week. Everything else — who drafted them, who started them, who picked them up — is simulated.

  1. Build the season. Real weekly box scores are re-scored under ESPN PPR rules, including kickers by distance and defences by points allowed. Preseason projections and average draft position come from what was actually published that August.
  2. Draft. Twelve managers run a 16-round snake from a randomised draft order. Each follows a playbook as a tendency: it nudges close calls, and clear value overrides it.
  3. Play the season. Every week: waiver claims in priority order, free-agent pickups, injured-reserve moves, then lineups set from what was knowable that Wednesday. Head-to-head against a randomised schedule.
  4. Playoffs. Six teams, seeded, through to a final — in the right weeks for that year’s NFL calendar, which is not the same before and after 2021.
  5. Repeat, then compare. For causal questions we replay identical leagues and change exactly one thing, so the difference cannot be luck.

The rule that makes it worth anything

No manager ever sees the future. The hardest part of building this was not the simulation — it was stopping hindsight leaking in. Projection files, for example, quietly zero out a player’s weekly projection for games he ended up missing, including the week he got hurt mid-game. Left alone, that hands every simulated manager a perfect injury forecast. Finding and repairing leaks like that is most of what the code does.

A manager who is injured mid-week still starts. A breakout is only visible after it has happened. A player on a bye is on a bye.

The search for something better

We went looking for a strategy nobody plays

If no fixed playbook survives, maybe the answer is a manager who reacts. We built two and tested them the hard way — identical leagues, one team switched, compared against that same team’s own behaviour.

One: react to the draft in front of you

Every manager in the simulation judges who will still be available at their next pick using preseason rankings — a number fixed before the draft began. So nobody notices a run. We built one who does: it estimates who survives from the pace of picks actually happening, and moves when a position starts emptying.

Worth +2.7 points a season, positive in four seasons of five.

Two: react to the season in front of you

August cannot see what kind of season it is. The first month largely can. How much better early running backs are paying off than early receivers, measured over weeks 1–6, predicts the rest of the season at +0.83 across the seven seasons.

An earlier version of this page put that figure at +0.98 and added that it held at +0.84 among players still healthy at week 6, “so it is not just the same absences counted twice.” Two more seasons pulled the headline number down, and the healthy-players check turns out never to have been statistically significant at either sample size. It leans that way, but it does not settle the objection, and it was written as though it did.

So we built a manager who drafts with no positional plan and then, from week 4, shops toward whatever is actually working. Worth +2.1 points a season.

Why neither one is the answer

Both are real — a careful experiment detects them. Both are tiny. Here they are against the thing that was already in the data. (These two tests cover five of the seven seasons; the 2021 and 2022 runs are still in progress.)

There is a reason the second one captures so little, and it is the most useful sentence on this page. The information arrives after the decision it would inform. Sixteen picks are allocated in August and then locked. The signal resolves in October. The only lever still available is a waiver wire offering marginal players.

We checked whether leaning harder helped. Tripling the in-season tilt made the good season slightly worse and the bad season catastrophic — from −6 points to −47. The downside scales; the upside does not. A strong positional tilt starts refusing genuinely good players for playing an unfashionable position.

The edge from being cleverer at the draft is about a tenth of the edge from paying attention after it.

We spent considerably more effort finding the first one.

The honest part

What this can’t tell you

  • Seven seasons is not many. Every “average across seasons” on this page rests on seven numbers. That is why every interval in the main table still crosses zero, and why we have reported that rather than picking a winner.
  • Strategy explains about 10% of the variance. Playbook, quarterback timing and manager effort together account for roughly a tenth of how much a team scores. The other 90% is which players got hurt, which unknowns broke out, and who you drew each week. Rank twelve teams by strategy alone and the agreement with how they actually finish averages 0.21 — swinging by ±0.29, so a completely backwards prediction is a normal outcome.
  • This is one league format. 12-team, full PPR, one quarterback, ESPN scoring and roster slots. Superflex, half-PPR and deeper benches will behave differently, and the quarterback conclusions especially would not survive a second starting quarterback.
  • The playbook mix is an assumption. How common each strategy is was set from published draft advice, not from any real league. Every finding was re-tested against different mixes, but the field you actually play in is not this field.
  • 2021 and 2022 use a different projection source. Their ESPN files were never obtainable, so they are built from nflverse box scores plus FantasyPros consensus. We tested that rather than assuming it: rebuilding 2024 without its ESPN file reproduces the ESPN strategy ranking at +0.98, closer than a season agrees with itself. 2020 is excluded deliberately, and before 2018 no weekly projection data exists anywhere we could find, which sets a hard floor on how far back this can go.

The advice shifts the odds. It does not decide the season.

A manager who follows everything here will still lose most years, and the one who ignores all of it will sometimes win. That is fantasy football, and it is most of the reason people play.

Simulated with real 2018–2025 scoring, ESPN projections and Fantasy Football Calculator draft data. 35,000 leagues, 420,000 team-seasons.
